## The desk that started it all

My desk had reached a point where I couldn't see the wood anymore. Papers, cups, cables, a plant I kept forgetting to water. Every time I sat down to work, I'd glance at the mess, feel a small wave of guilt, and open my laptop anyway. Tomorrow. I'd deal with it tomorrow.

Instead of cleaning the desk, I thought about *why* I kept avoiding it. It wasn't that the task was hard — it's a desk, not a demolition job. It was that "clean the desk" felt like one giant, undefined chore with no clear start and no visible finish line. Overwhelming in a way that had nothing to do with the actual amount of work.

But here's the thing about actually tidying something, once you finally start: it never stays overwhelming for long. You pick up one cup. Then another. You find a home for the cables. Bit by bit, the desk gets smaller as a problem, and bigger as a finished thing. And then — it's done. You can see the wood again. That moment, right there, is disproportionately satisfying for how little "work" it technically was.

I wanted to put that feeling into a typing game.

## Meet Tidy

[Tidy](/games/tidy) drops a room full of words onto your screen — a pile, literally, with real physics, stacking and tumbling the way actual clutter does. Type a word correctly and it's gone, lifted off the pile and out of the way. That's the whole game.

No clock. No score counter demanding your attention. No combo multiplier yelling at you to go faster. Just you, the keys, and a room that needs tidying.

## Why no rush

Most typing games — including several of ours — run on a timer, and that's a completely valid kind of fun. Type Rush and TypeFall want your fingers moving fast under pressure, and we're upfront about that.

Tidy is built around a different feeling: **craft**. Think about sanding a table, or weeding a garden bed, or actually tidying a desk. Nobody's standing over you with a stopwatch. You work steadily, you can see your progress the whole time, and the satisfaction isn't one big payoff at the end — it builds up, word by word, the longer you work at it.

So the mechanics are built to protect that feeling:

- **The room actually narrows as you go.** Clear a word and Tidy doesn't throw a random new word at you — it duplicates a word that's *already on the pile*. That means the longer you play, the fewer distinct words remain, and the easier the room gets to finish. Most games ramp difficulty up near the end. Tidy ramps it down, on purpose, because that's what real tidying feels like: the job gets easier to finish the more you've already done.
- **You pick the size of the job.** Typing a number from 30 to 500 before a round starts is you deciding how big a room you're taking on today — one shelf, or the whole thing.
- **There's a real ending.** When the last word clears, the room actually empties. The screen breathes for a second, and then — `clarity`. The moment the clutter is gone and you can finally see the room again, in sunlight.

## The part that surprised me

I expected the appeal to be the physics — watching a pile of words wobble and settle is weirdly mesmerizing on its own. But the part that actually kept me playing was the narrowing. Somewhere around the last ten words of a 200-word room, you realize you're clearing the *same handful of words* over and over, faster and faster, and the pile is visibly collapsing toward zero. It feels like the home stretch of actually finishing something. That's the desk-is-clean feeling, except it only takes two minutes instead of an afternoon of guilt.

We've since written up the thinking behind this pacing as an internal design guide, because it's different enough from our timed games that it deserves its own rules — not "no timer," but a whole different relationship between the player and the finish line.

## The wall I keep hitting

I've been learning Spanish on Duolingo for a while. It works, in the way it's supposed to: I picked up the grammar basics. I know how the present tense bends, roughly where the verb goes, why *estoy* and *soy* are different things.

And then I open a real sentence and understand almost none of it.

It isn't the grammar. I can see the shape of the sentence just fine. I just don't know the words that fill it. A sentence with eight words, three of which I know, isn't "mostly understood." It's noise with a few landmarks. Grammar tells you how words fit together; it can't help if you're missing the words.

Vocabulary is the bottleneck, and Duolingo, for me, drips it out slowly, one themed unit at a time.

## The idea that showed up while I was playing

I was playing [Tidy](/games/tidy), our calm no-clock typing game, when I noticed something about how it works. You clear a word, and instead of a fresh random one, the pile *duplicates a word that's already there*. By the end of a room you're typing the same small handful of words over and over, faster each time.

That's repetition. It's the thing vocabulary needs and the thing I wasn't getting enough of.

So I cloned the game into Spanish: [Tidy en español](/games/tidy-es). Same physics, same no-rush room, but the pile is made of the most common Spanish words.

## How it's built

- **The 3000 most common words.** I used a public frequency list of the most-used Spanish words (it's built from movie and TV subtitles, so it's everyday spoken Spanish, not textbook Spanish) and kept the words of three letters or more, minus character names. Very short words like *a* and *y* clear the instant you press a key, which makes for a boring game. That's 3000 words, each with an English gloss.
- **Common first, on purpose.** If you only learn a few hundred words, the most frequent ones cover a huge share of what you'll actually read and hear. That's the best deal in vocabulary.
- **Accents optional.** The words are shown properly (*está*, *más*, *señor*), but you type them without accents. Typing `esta` clears *está*. I wanted the game to teach me the words, not fight my keyboard.
- **It remembers what you've cleared.** Your cleared words are saved in your browser, and new rooms deal you words you *haven't* cleared yet first. You don't get stuck re-clearing *que* for the tenth time while hundreds of words sit untouched. Once you've cleared all of them, the list resets and you start another lap.

## The meaning line

Every time you clear a word, a short line appears under the room with its English meaning: `casa  =  house, home`. It stays until you clear the next one, so there's time to read it without breaking the no-rush feel.

The glosses are quick, one-line translations, not a dictionary. Some words have several meanings (*tarde* is both "late" and "afternoon"), and the line only shows the main one or two.

## The honest limitation

Typing a word is still not the same as knowing it. The game gives you exposure and repetition, plus the meaning every time you clear a word. Whether it sticks is up to you, and that's where Duolingo comes in.

That's the pitch, really. Duolingo for grammar and meaning in context. Tidy for volume and repetition on the words that matter most. One doesn't replace the other.

## The experiment

I don't know yet if this works. That's the point of writing it down.

Here's my test: keep doing my Duolingo lessons, add a few rooms of Tidy en español a day, and check, a few weeks from now, whether real Spanish sentences have gotten less noisy. Fewer gaps, more landmarks. If the common words start jumping out at me when I read, it's working. If not, I'll say so.

If you're learning Spanish, I'd love to know if it helps you too. It's free, there's no clock, and the worst case is a few calm minutes of typing.
